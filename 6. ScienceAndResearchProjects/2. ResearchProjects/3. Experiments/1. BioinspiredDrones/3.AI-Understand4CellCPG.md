## 🎯 What is the Big Idea?

Have you ever wondered how you walk without thinking, *"Okay brain, lift left foot, move forward, put down, now lift right foot..."*? Or how a horse switches from a slow walk to a fast gallop? Or how a sea slug swims smoothly through the ocean?

Animals have tiny mini-brains inside their spinal cords called **CPGs (Central Pattern Generators)**. Think of a CPG as an automatic `while(True)` loop running in the background of your body! Once you turn it on, it creates repeating **rhythms** all by itself—like a musical drum machine for your muscles.

In our project, we are building a **4-Cell CPG** in code. Why 4 cells? Because a quadcopter drone has **4 propellers**! By connecting each virtual brain cell to one propeller, we can make our drone rock back and forth, spin, or swim through the air like a living creature just by changing a few function parameters!

## 🧩 Part 1: The Building Blocks (Neuroscience for Coders)

### 1. What is a Neuron (Brain Cell)?

To a programmer, a **neuron** is just an **Object** with two main variables that change over time in a game loop:

* **`V` (Voltage / Energy):** How excited the cell is right now. If $V \ge 0$, the cell is **ON (Active / Firing)**. In our drone, when `V` is high, the propeller spins fast! If $V < 0$, the cell is **OFF (Resting)**.
* **`x` (Recovery / Tiredness):** Think of this like a stamina bar in a video game. When the cell turns ON (`V` is high), it slowly gets tired (`x` goes up). Once it gets too tired, it forces `V` to drop back down to rest. While resting, its tiredness `x` goes back down until it is ready to fire again!

### 2. How do Neurons Talk? (Inhibitory Synapses)

Neurons are connected by wires called **Synapses**. In this paper, all the connections are **Inhibitory** (drawn as lines ending with a little circle $\bullet$). 

* **What "Inhibitory" means in code:** It’s like a ** Freeze Ray** or a **"Shush!" button**. When Cell 1 turns ON, it shoots a freeze ray at the cells it is connected to, pushing their Voltage (`V`) down so they stay OFF.
* **Why does this make rhythms?** Imagine two kids (Cell 1 and Cell 2) who both want to talk, but whenever Cell 1 talks, it holds down a mute button on Cell 2. Eventually, Cell 1 gets tired (`x` gets too high) and has to stop talking to catch its breath. The moment Cell 1 stops, Cell 2 is unmuted and starts talking—which mutes Cell 1! They end up taking turns forever: *1, 2, 1, 2, 1, 2*. Scientists call a 2-cell turn-taker a **Half-Center Oscillator (HCO)**.

### 3. Phase Lags ($\Delta\theta$): Measuring Who Goes When

When 4 cells are firing in a repeating loop, how do we tell the computer what pattern they are making? We use **Phase Lags** (written as $\Delta\theta_{12}, \Delta\theta_{13}, \Delta\theta_{14}$).

Think of a clock or a circular race track from `0.0` to `1.0` (which is $0\%$ to $100\%$ of one full cycle):

* We pick **Cell 1** as our leader (the stopwatch starter). Every time Cell 1 turns ON, our timer resets to `0.0`.
* **$\Delta\theta_{12}$** is simply: *"How far through Cell 1's cycle did Cell 2 turn ON?"*
  * **`0.0` (or `1.0`):** **In-Phase (Synchronized)!** They turn ON at the exact same time. (Because it's a circle, `0.0` and `1.0` are the exact same spot, just like `angle % 1.0` in Python!).
  * **`0.5`:** **Anti-Phase (Exact Opposites)!** Cell 2 waits until Cell 1 is halfway done ($50\%$) before turning ON.
  * **`0.25`, `0.5`, `0.75`:** **Stepping Stones!** Cell 2 turns on at $25\%$, Cell 3 at $50\%$, and Cell 4 at $75\%$.

So for a 4-cell network, we can describe ANY rhythm using a 3-number tuple: **`(lag_12, lag_13, lag_14)`**!

### 4. What is "Multistability" and an "Attractor Basin"?

* **Multistability:** Imagine a toy robot that can walk, hop, OR spin—all using the *exact same code and parameters*, depending only on how you push it when you turn it on! In code, this means keeping the function parameters fixed, but changing the **Initial Conditions** (the starting values of `V` and `x` at `t = 0`) gives you different stable rhythms.
* **Attractor (Fixed Point):** A stable rhythm that the cells lock into and repeat forever.
* **Attraction Basin:** Imagine a bumpy field with 3 deep bowls (attractors) painted Red, Green, and Blue. If you drop a marble anywhere in the field, it will roll down into one of the bowls. The **Attraction Basin** is the zone of starting positions that roll into a specific bowl. If the Orange basin takes up $80\%$ of the map, then $80\%$ of random starting values will end up doing the Orange rhythm!
* **Bifurcation:** A fancy math word for a **"Pattern Switch"**. When you slowly slide a parameter knob (like `I_app`) and suddenly a rhythm disappears or transforms into a brand new rhythm!

## 💻 Part 2: The Math Equations as a Code Function

In Section 2 of the paper, the authors show Equation (1), which is the **FitzHugh-Nagumo neuron model**. Don't let the calculus symbols ($\frac{dV}{dt}$) scare you! In programming, $\frac{dV}{dt}$ just means `change_in_V_per_frame`!

Here is what the math in the paper looks like, translated directly into Python-style pseudocode:

### The Math in the Paper:

$$\frac{dV_i}{dt} = V_i - V_i^3 - x_i + I_{app} + \sum_{j \neq i} g_{inh}^{ji} G(V_i, V_j)$$
$$\frac{dx_i}{dt} = \epsilon \left[ x_{\infty}(V_i) - x_i \right]$$

### How to Read It Like a Programmer:

Think of the whole 4-cell network as a function that updates every tiny time step `dt`:

```python
def update_cpg_network(V, x, I_app, g_inh, epsilon, connections):
    """
    Inputs (State Variables - Arrays of length 4 for our 4 cells):
      V : Current voltages of cells [V1, V2, V3, V4]
      x : Current tiredness/recovery of cells [x1, x2, x3, x4]

    Function Parameters (The Control Knobs from the Paper!):
      I_app       : External Drive (Gas pedal / battery power, between 0.4 and 0.6)
      g_inh       : Synaptic Strength (How strong the "freeze ray" is, between 0.005 and 0.03)
      epsilon     : Time-scale speed (How fast cells get tired and recover, e.g., 0.05, 0.3, 0.5)
      connections : A 4x4 grid (matrix) of 1s and 0s showing which cell inhibits which cell
    """
```

Let’s break down the helper formulas inside that function:

1. **The Tiredness Target ($x_{\infty}$):**
   $$x_{\infty}(V_i) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-10 V_i}}$$
   *In code:* This is a **Sigmoid function** (a smooth S-shaped switch between `0` and `1`). If Voltage $V_i$ is positive (Cell is ON), $x_{\infty}$ becomes `1.0` (telling tiredness `x` to grow toward `1`). If $V_i$ is negative (Cell is OFF), $x_{\infty}$ becomes `0.0` (telling tiredness `x` to drop back to `0`).
2. **The Freeze Ray Switch ($\Gamma(V_j)$ and $G(V_i, V_j)$):**
   $$\Gamma(V_j) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-100 V_j}}, \quad G(V_i, V_j) = (-1.5 - V_i) \cdot \Gamma(V_j)$$
   *In code:* $\Gamma(V_j)$ checks if neighbor cell $j$ is ON ($V_j > 0$). Because of the `-100`, it acts almost like an `if V[j] > 0: return 1.0 else: return 0.0` statement! When neighbor $j$ is ON, it pulls cell $i$'s voltage down toward $E_{rev} = -1.5$, multiplied by the freeze-ray strength `g_inh`.

### 🎛️ The 4 Control Knobs (Your Function Parameters!)

When you run `tracesTest_4Cell.py`, you control the rhythms by changing 4 things:

1. **`I_app` (External Drive, `0.4` to `0.575`):** The gas pedal! Below `0.4`, the cells fall asleep. Above `0.6`, they fire non-stop. Between `0.4` and `0.575`, they make awesome bursts!
2. **`g_inh` (Inhibition Strength, `0.005` to `0.03`):** How hard the cells push each other down. Weak push vs. super strong push changes who wins!
3. **`epsilon` ($\epsilon$, Recovery Speed, `0.05` to `0.5`):** How fast the stamina bar `x` changes compared to voltage `V`. Small $\epsilon$ (`0.05`) means slow stamina changes; larger $\epsilon$ (`0.5`) means fast stamina changes.
4. **Network Topology (Wiring Diagram):** Which cells are wired together!

## 🕺 Part 3: Meet the 7 Rhythms (And What They Do to a Drone!)

When we connect Cell 1, Cell 2, Cell 3, and Cell 4 to our 4 drone propellers, each rhythm creates a unique flight dance! Look at **Figure 5** on Page 8 to see the wavy lines (voltage traces) for each one:

1. **🟠 Paired Half-Center `(0.5, 0.0, 0.5)` or `(0.0, 0.5, 0.5)` or `(0.5, 0.5, 0.0)`**
   
   * **What the cells do:** The 4 cells team up into 2 pairs! For example, **(Cell 1 & Cell 3)** fire together, while **(Cell 2 & Cell 4)** rest. Then they swap!
   * **Why there are 3 versions ("Isomorphisms"):** "Isomorphism" just means "same pattern, different teammates." Cell 1 can team up with Cell 2, Cell 3, or Cell 4.
   * **Drone Flight:** Two propellers spin fast while two spin slow, then they switch! This makes the drone rock back-and-forth (pitch) or side-to-side (roll) like a seesaw!

2. **🔵 Pacemaker `(0.5, 0.5, 0.5)` (and 3 other versions)**
   
   * **What the cells do:** One boss cell (the Pacemaker, say Cell 1) fires all by itself. While it fires, it pushes the other THREE cells down. When the boss rests, all 3 other cells fire together at the exact same time!
   * **Why there are 4 versions:** Any of the 4 cells can be the boss!
   * **Drone Flight:** One corner dips/lifts, then the other three push back together!

3. **🟢 Synchronized State `(0.0, 0.0, 0.0)`**
   
   * **What the cells do:** Surprise! Even though all 4 cells are shooting freeze rays at each other, if the parameters are *just right*, all 4 cells turn ON and OFF at the exact same time!
   * **Drone Flight:** All 4 propellers speed up and slow down together—making the drone bounce up and down vertically like it’s jumping on a trampoline!

4. **🟣 Full Traveling Wave `(0.25, 0.5, 0.75)` (and 5 other versions)**
   
   * **What the cells do:** Like "The Wave" in a sports stadium! Cell 1 fires $\rightarrow$ then Cell 2 $\rightarrow$ then Cell 3 $\rightarrow$ then Cell 4 $\rightarrow$ back to Cell 1! Each cell waits $25\%$ (`0.25`) of a cycle after the previous one.
   * **Why there are 6 versions:** With 4 cells, there are $3 \times 2 \times 1 = 6$ different orders they can take turns in (e.g., Clockwise `1-2-3-4`, Counter-Clockwise `1-4-3-2`, or zig-zag `1-3-2-4`!).
   * **Drone Flight:** The propellers boost in a circle around the drone body, making the drone wobble/swirl in a smooth circular motion!

5. **🩷 Mixed Traveling Wave `(0.0, 0.67, 0.33)` (12 versions, seen at $\epsilon = 0.05$)**
   
   * **What the cells do:** Two cells team up and fire together (say Cell 1 & 2 at `0.0`), followed by Cell 4 at `0.33` ($1/3$ of the cycle), followed by Cell 3 at `0.67` ($2/3$ of the cycle). It's a 3-step wave using 4 cells!

6. **⚪ Transition Rhythm `(0.41, 0.5, 0.91)`**
   
   * **What the cells do:** A stable "in-between" state when a Full Traveling Wave is morphing into a Paired Half-Center!

7. **⚫ Chimera State `(11:10 Resonance)`**
   
   * **What the cells do:** In Greek mythology, a Chimera is a monster made of different animals. In CPGs, a Chimera is when 3 cells lock together and fire in perfect sync, while the 4th cell goes rogue and fires at a slower speed! Every 10 cycles of the rogue cell, the 3 synced cells finish 11 cycles, and they briefly line up again (`11:10`).

### 📌 Figure 2 (Page 5): The 4 Wiring Diagrams (Network Topologies)

When we have 4 cells, how should we wire them up? To keep things fair (**Homogeneous**), every cell must receive the exact same number of incoming freeze-rays:

* **Fig. 2(a) One-Way Loop:** 1 inhibits 2 $\rightarrow$ 2 inhibits 3 $\rightarrow$ 3 inhibits 4 $\rightarrow$ 4 inhibits 1. (Every cell gets **1** input).
* **Fig. 2(b) Two-Way Loop:** Cells inhibit their neighbors on both sides around the square, but NOT across the middle diagonals. (Every cell gets **2** inputs).
* **Fig. 2(c) Mixed Network:** A One-Way Loop around the outside PLUS Two-Way connections across the middle X diagonals! (Every cell gets **2** inputs). *Fun fact: This matches the swimming brain of the sea slug Melibe leonina!*
* **Fig. 2(d) Fully Connected:** Every cell inhibits all 3 other cells! (Every cell gets **3** inputs).

### 📌 Figure 3 & Table 1 (Pages 5–6): The 3D Sliced Cube & Machine Learning!

* **Why is Fig. 3 a 3D box?** In a 3-cell network (Fig. 1e), we only had 2 phase lags ($\Delta\theta_{12}, \Delta\theta_{13}$), which fit on a flat 2D square. With **4 cells**, we have **3 phase lags** ($\Delta\theta_{12}, \Delta\theta_{13}, \Delta\theta_{14}$)! So our map becomes a **3D Cube** ($25 \times 25 \times 25 = 15,625$ starting points!).
* Looking inside a solid 3D cube is hard, so the authors sliced it like a loaf of bread to show the Red, Green, and Blue attraction basins inside (`Paired Half-Centers` at $g_{inh}=0.025, I_{app}=0.575, \epsilon=0.5$).
* **Why did they use Machine Learning (Clustering)?** Instead of a human staring at 3D cubes for thousands of hours, they used an **Unsupervised Machine Learning Clustering Algorithm**! The computer automatically groups all trajectories that end up at the same `(lag_12, lag_13, lag_14)` coordinate and prints out **Table 1**:
  * **CCM (Cluster Circular Mean):** The `(lag_12, lag_13, lag_14)` rhythm of that group!
  * **CCSD (Cluster Circular Standard Deviation):** How noisy or wobbly the group is (`0.0` means rock-solid!).
  * **PC (Percentage of Convergence):** What $\%$ of the $15,625$ starting points rolled into that rhythm! In Table 1, each of the 3 Paired Half-Centers gets about $33.3\%$ of the cube.

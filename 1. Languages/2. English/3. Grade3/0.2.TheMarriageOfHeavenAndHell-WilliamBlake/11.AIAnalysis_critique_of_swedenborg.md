# Comprehensive Study Guide: Section 11 – Critique of Swedenborg and Systematic Reasoning

**From William Blake’s *The Marriage of Heaven and Hell* (Plates 21–22)**

*(Note on Section 11: Immediately following the fourth "Memorable Fancy"—where Blake showed the Angel that his orthodox metaphysics were nothing more than a house of chained monkeys and the dry skeleton of Aristotle’s "Analytics"—Blake steps forward to deliver a direct, scorching critique of the Swedish theologian and mystic **Emanuel Swedenborg** \[1688–1772\]. Having briefly attended a New Jerusalem Church conference in London in 1789, Blake had grown deeply frustrated with Swedenborg’s followers and with Swedenborg’s own spiritual pride. In this section, Blake contrasts mechanical, derivative system-building \[which merely indexes other people's ideas\] with true, blazing Poetic Genius \[represented by Paracelsus, Jakob Böhme, Dante, and Shakespeare\].)*

## Sentence-by-Sentence Breakdown

### Sentence 1

> **Original Text:**
> "“I have always found that Angels have the vanity to speak of themselves as the only wise; this they do with a confident insolence sprouting from systematic reasoning."

* **Sentence Translation and Detailed Implied Meaning (For Adults):**
  *Translation:* "In all my experience, I have consistently observed that conventional, rule-bound moralists and theologians ('Angels') possess the arrogant conceit ('vanity') to declare that they alone possess true wisdom; and they assert this superiority with a smug, overbearing rudeness ('confident insolence') that grows directly out of their reliance on rigid, step-by-step rational systems ('systematic reasoning')."
  *Implied Meaning:* Blake diagnoses the specific psychological arrogance of dogmatists and rationalists. Why did the Angel in the previous section speak to Blake with such condescending superiority (*"O pitiable foolish young man!"*)? Because **"systematic reasoning"**—building a closed, neatly organized box of rules and deductions—gives small-minded thinkers the illusion of omniscience. Once a thinker has constructed a tidy system where every question has a pre-packaged answer, they mistake the neatness of their system for absolute truth, breeding "confident insolence" toward anyone whose imagination overflows their categories.

* **Detailed Explanation (For an 8-Year-Old):**
  After finishing his adventure with the strict Angel in the last chapter, Blake tells us something he has noticed his whole life: bossy rule-followers (**"Angels"**) are always bragging that *they* are the only smart, wise people in the room!
  Why do they act so snooty and rude (**"confident insolence"**)? Because they love **"systematic reasoning"**—which means making giant checklists, charts, and strict rule-systems for everything. Because they memorized their own rulebook, they think they know everything in the universe and look down on everyone else!

### Sentence 2

> **Original Text:**
> "“Thus Swedenborg boasts that what he writes is new; though it is only the contents or index of already published books."

* **Sentence Translation and Detailed Implied Meaning (For Adults):**
  *Translation:* "In this exact manner, Emanuel Swedenborg proudly claims that his theological writings contain brand-new divine revelations, when in reality his books are nothing more than a rearranged table of contents or alphabetical index of books that have already been written and published by earlier thinkers."
  *Implied Meaning:* Swedenborg trained for decades as a scientist, mathematician, and assessor of mines before turning to theology, and he carried that classifying, cataloging habit into his spiritual writings. In works like *True Christian Religion* and *Heaven and Hell*, Swedenborg claimed to unveil a brand-new dispensation of truth never before known to humanity. Blake punctures this boast with a devastating literary metaphor: Swedenborg hasn't written a new *book* of living vision; he has merely compiled the **"contents or index"** (an analytical summary and classification) of older mystical and biblical texts! (Recall the Devourer in Section 9 and the men in the sixth chamber of the Printing-House who merely arrange existing books in libraries.)

* **Detailed Explanation (For an 8-Year-Old):**
  Blake points at the famous writer **Swedenborg** (whom we met back in Section 1 sitting beside the empty tomb). Swedenborg loved to brag: *"Look at my giant books! Everything I wrote is brand-new and nobody ever thought of it before!"*
  Blake laughs and says: *"No, it isn't!"* Do you know what the **Table of Contents** or the **Index** at the back of a book is? It isn't the real story—it is just a list of page numbers and topics that somebody else already wrote! Blake says that Swedenborg didn't invent any new stories or wisdom; he just copied and organized a list of ideas from older books that were already in the library!

### Sentence 3

> **Original Text:**
> "“A man carried a monkey about for a show, and because he was a little wiser than the monkey, grew vain, and conceived himself as much wiser than seven men."

* **Sentence Translation and Detailed Implied Meaning (For Adults):**
  *Translation:* "Consider this parable: A street performer carried a pet monkey around town to put on a public show, and simply because he was slightly more intelligent than his monkey, he became puffed up with pride and deluded himself into believing that he was wiser than seven grown men combined."
  *Implied Meaning:* Picking up the image of the chained monkeys from the seven brick houses in Section 10, Blake invents a witty satirical fable that also echoes the biblical Book of Proverbs (Proverbs 26:16: *"The sluggard is wiser in his own conceit than seven men that can render a reason"*). Traveling monkey-shows were a common sight in eighteenth-century London streets. By comparing himself only to a captive animal on a chain, the showman easily feels like a towering genius—forgetting how ridiculous his arrogance looks when measured against genuinely wise human beings ("seven men").

* **Detailed Explanation (For an 8-Year-Old):**
  Blake tells a funny little story to show how silly it is to brag:
  Imagine a man walking down the street carrying a little pet **monkey** on his shoulder to put on a show. Because the man knows how to tie his shoes and count coins—making him a tiny bit smarter than his pet monkey—he puffs out his chest, gets super conceited ("grew vain"), and thinks to himself: *"Wow, look how much smarter I am than this monkey! That means I must be smarter than **seven wise grown-ups** put together!"*

### Sentence 4

> **Original Text:**
> "It is so with Swedenborg; he shows the folly of churches, and exposes hypocrites, till he imagines that all are religious, and himself the single one on earth that ever broke a net."

* **Sentence Translation and Detailed Implied Meaning (For Adults):**
  *Translation:* "It is the exact same case with Swedenborg: he rightly points out the foolishness of orthodox churches and unmasks religious hypocrites, until he grows so vain that he imagines the entire human race is trapped in conventional religion and that he alone is the only person on earth who ever broke free from the snare ('broke a net')."
  *Implied Meaning:* Blake gives credit where credit is due: Swedenborg genuinely did expose the corruption of institutional churches and the hypocrisy of dead orthodoxy (which is why Blake initially read him with interest). However, seeing through the obvious foolishness of corrupt church hypocrites is no harder than being "a little wiser than the monkey"! Because Swedenborg spent all his time comparing himself to narrow-minded church dogmatists, he fell into the trap of thinking that *everyone* on earth was blind except him, and that he was the first person in history to break out of the "net" of institutional deceit (recall Proverb 13: *"All wholesome food is caught without a net or a trap"*). In doing so, he completely ignored the great tradition of free, visionary poets and prophets who had already broken that net centuries before him.

* **Detailed Explanation (For an 8-Year-Old):**
  Blake says that **Swedenborg** acted just like that man with the monkey!
  Swedenborg looked around at the grumpy, hypocritical rule-makers in the churches and pointed out their silly mistakes. That part was good—but pointing out that hypocrites are silly is as easy as being smarter than a monkey! Because Swedenborg did that one easy thing, he got puffed up with pride and thought: *"Everyone else in the whole world is trapped in a giant net of silly rules, and I am the ONLY person on Earth smart enough to break out of the net!"*

### Sentence 5

> **Original Text:**
> "“Now hear a plain fact: Swedenborg has not written one new truth. Now hear another: he has written all the old falsehoods."

* **Sentence Translation and Detailed Implied Meaning (For Adults):**
  *Translation:* "Now listen to a straightforward, undeniable fact: Swedenborg has not contributed a single original truth to human knowledge. And now listen to a second fact: he has repeated every single one of the old, oppressive errors of orthodox religion."
  *Implied Meaning:* With punchy, prophetic directness, Blake strips away Swedenborg’s reputation as a radical reformer. Whatever is *true* in Swedenborg’s books (such as the symbolic nature of scripture or the critique of faith without charity) was already taught long ago by earlier mystics and poets, so it is not "new." Worse still, because Swedenborg refused to abandon moralistic dualism—still believing in an eternal Hell of punishment for sinners and praising passive obedience to celestial authority—he ended up repeating **"all the old falsehoods"** (the very three errors of "All Bibles or sacred codes" exposed in *The Voice of the Devil*!).

* **Detailed Explanation (For an 8-Year-Old):**
  Blake says: *"Let me tell you two plain, simple facts about Swedenborg's books!"*

  * **Plain Fact #1:** Swedenborg didn't write even **one** brand-new truth that wise poets hadn't already shared long ago.

  * **Plain Fact #2:** Even worse, he copied down **all the old mistakes ("falsehoods")**—like saying that big Energy is bad and that people should be scared of punishment!

### Sentence 6

> **Original Text:**
> "“And now hear the reason: he conversed with Angels who are all religious, and conversed not with Devils who all hate religion, for he was incapable through his conceited notions."

* **Sentence Translation and Detailed Implied Meaning (For Adults):**
  *Translation:* "And now listen to the reason why Swedenborg failed: in his visions, he spoke exclusively with 'Angels'—who are all pious, orthodox conformists—and refused to converse with 'Devils,' who all despise institutional religion; for his own self-righteous arrogance ('conceited notions') rendered him incapable of engaging with rebellious creative Energy."
  *Implied Meaning:* This is the satirical key to why Swedenborg’s *Heaven and Hell* failed where Blake’s *Marriage of Heaven and Hell* succeeds. Swedenborg famously claimed that God allowed him to talk daily with angels in Heaven and spirits in Hell. However, because Swedenborg approached the spiritual world with "conceited notions" of conventional morality, he only listened to the respectable **"Angels"** (the voices of passive Reason and pious orthodoxy) while treating the **"Devils"** (the voices of passionate Energy, genius, and rebellion against institutional religion) as monsters to be shunned! Because *"Without contraries is no progression,"* listening only to Angels while silencing Devils traps a thinker in one-sided falsehood.

* **Detailed Explanation (For an 8-Year-Old):**
  Why did Swedenborg make such a big mistake? Blake explains the reason: when Swedenborg went on his imagination trips, he *only* talked to the polite, rule-following **"Angels,"** and he refused to listen to the fiery, creative **"Devils"** (the brave artists and big thinkers who hate bossy rules)! Because Swedenborg thought he was too good and too proper ("his conceited notions") to talk with Team Energy, he only heard half of the story!

### Sentence 7

> **Original Text:**
> "“Thus Swedenborg’s writings are a recapitulation of all superficial opinions, and an analysis of the more sublime, but no further."

* **Sentence Translation and Detailed Implied Meaning (For Adults):**
  *Translation:* "Consequently, Swedenborg’s voluminous books amount to nothing more than a repetitive summary ('recapitulation') of conventional, surface-level moral opinions, combined with a dry, dissecting breakdown ('an analysis') of deeper, awe-inspiring spiritual truths ('the more sublime')—going no further than mere analysis."
  *Implied Meaning:* Remember at the end of Section 10 when Blake told the Angel, *"it is but lost time to converse with you whose works are only **Analytics**"*? Here Blake applies that exact diagnosis to Swedenborg. Swedenborg does two things:

  1. He repeats **"superficial opinions"** (conventional moralizing about good and evil).

  2. When he encounters genuinely **"sublime"** (deep, visionary, poetic) truths from earlier mystics and prophets, he subjects them to **"an analysis"**—dissecting, numbering, and categorizing them like a scientist pinning dead butterflies to a board, rather than soaring with them into new creation ("but no further").

* **Detailed Explanation (For an 8-Year-Old):**
  Because Swedenborg only listened to the strict Angels, his big books only do two things:

  1. First, they repeat ("recapitulate") all the ordinary, surface-level rules that everyone already heard in church.

  2. Second, when he finds a truly giant, magical idea ("the more sublime"), instead of flying with it, he just chops it up into a dry science report (**"an analysis"**) with numbers and labels—and never goes a single step further!

### Sentence 8

> **Original Text:**
> "“Have now another plain fact: any man of mechanical talents may from the writings of Paracelsus or Jacob Behmen produce ten thousand volumes of equal value with Swedenborg’s, and from those of Dante or Shakespeare an infinite number."

* **Sentence Translation and Detailed Implied Meaning (For Adults):**
  *Translation:* "Now hear one more plain fact: any person possessing ordinary, methodical, rule-following skills ('mechanical talents') could take the visionary writings of Paracelsus or Jakob Böhme and crank out ten thousand volumes just as good as Swedenborg’s—and from the supreme poetic works of Dante or Shakespeare, they could generate an infinite number!"
  *Implied Meaning:* Here Blake contrasts the derivative systematizer (who possesses mere **"mechanical talents"**—the ability to classify, summarize, and rearrange) with four of his greatest spiritual and artistic heroes—true **Prolific** geniuses who drank straight from the fountain of Hell/Energy:

  1. **Paracelsus (1493–1541) and Jacob Behmen / Jakob Böhme (1575–1624):** The great Renaissance physician-alchemist Paracelsus and the German visionary shoemaker Jakob Böhme were radical Christian mystics who taught that the microcosm of humanity contains the entire universe and that dynamic opposition (contraries of fire and light, wrath and love) is the engine of creation. Swedenborg borrowed heavily from their ideas without acknowledging them.

  2. **Dante Alighieri (1265–1321) and William Shakespeare (1564–1616):** Above even the mystics stand the supreme **Poets**—Dante (who courageously descended through the fires of the *Inferno*) and Shakespeare (whose boundless imagination encompassed every human passion). Because true poetry is **infinite**, any mechanical clerk could spend eternity writing analytical commentaries on Dante or Shakespeare without ever exhausting their depths!

* **Detailed Explanation (For an 8-Year-Old):**
  Blake gives us one more plain fact: you don't even need a big imagination to write books like Swedenborg’s—anyone with **"mechanical talents"** (someone who is just good at copying, sorting, and organizing things like a machine) could do it!
  In fact, if an organizer took the amazing, fiery books of great thinkers like **Paracelsus** or **Jakob Böhme**, they could easily write **ten thousand** books just like Swedenborg’s! And if they took the giant, magical plays and poems of **Dante** or **William Shakespeare**, they could write an **infinite (never-ending)** number of books just by summarizing them!

### Sentence 9

> **Original Text:**
> "“But when he has done this, let him not say that he knows better than his master, for he only holds a candle in sunshine.”"

* **Sentence Translation and Detailed Implied Meaning (For Adults):**
  *Translation:* "Yet after this mechanical writer has produced all those thousands of derivative volumes, let him not dare boast that he is wiser than the great visionary masters from whom he borrowed—for his rational analysis is merely holding up a tiny, flickering wax candle in the middle of the blazing midday sunshine!"
  *Implied Meaning:* Blake closes Section 11 with an unforgettable, proverbial image of intellectual humility versus rationalist hubris: **"for he only holds a candle in sunshine."**

  * **The Sunshine:** The blazing, self-luminous sun of the **Poetic Genius** (the original works of prophets and poets like Isaiah, Ezekiel, Böhme, Dante, and Shakespeare, or the living Sun into which Blake leaped in Section 10).

  * **The Candle:** The small, secondary, flickering light of analytical Reason, commentary, and systematic theology (like Swedenborg’s volumes).
    A candle is made from tallow or wax borrowed from living things, and while a tiny candle may seem impressive inside a dark cavern, holding up a little candle in the middle of blazing noon sunshine and bragging that your candle is brighter than the Sun is the height of folly!

* **Detailed Explanation (For an 8-Year-Old):**
  Blake ends this chapter with a picture that is so funny and clear you will never forget it!
  Imagine going outside at noon on a bright, sunny summer day when the giant golden **Sun** is lighting up the whole sky. Now imagine someone walking outside holding a tiny little **birthday candle**, puffing out their chest, and bragging: *"Look at my little candle! It is way brighter and better than the Sun!"*
  Blake says that great poets and creators (like Shakespeare and Dante) are the blazing **Sunshine** of imagination, while rule-makers and summarizers (like Swedenborg) are just holding up a tiny **candle in the sunshine**! Never mistake a little candle of rules for the giant Sun of true creativity!

## Comprehensive Summaries of Section 11: "Critique of Swedenborg"

### 1. Detailed Summary for Adults and Educators

In **Section 11 (Plates 21–22)**, William Blake transitions from his visionary encounter with the Angel into a direct, incisive literary and philosophical critique of **Emanuel Swedenborg**, exposing the limits of systematic theology and derivative rationalism:

* **The Vanity of Systematic Reasoning:** Blake observes that "Angels" (orthodox moralists and systematizers) speak with a "confident insolence" born from their reliance on closed, systematic reasoning. Although Swedenborg boasted that his theological system was a brand-new revelation, Blake exposes it as merely "the contents or index of already published books."

* **The Parable of the Monkey Showman:** Through the satirical fable of the showman who grew vain because he was slightly wiser than his pet monkey—imagining himself wiser than seven men—Blake explains Swedenborg’s spiritual blind spot. Because Swedenborg saw through the obvious hypocrisy of corrupt churches, he flattered himself that he alone had escaped the "net" of error. Yet because his own self-righteousness made him converse only with pious "Angels" while shunning rebellious "Devils" (creative Energy), he failed to write a single new truth and instead repeated all the old dualistic falsehoods.

* **Mechanical Talent vs. Poetic Genius (A Candle in Sunshine):** Swedenborg’s works are merely a summary of superficial moral opinions and a dissecting "analysis" of the sublime. Any writer with mere "mechanical talents" could extract ten thousand such volumes from great mystics like **Paracelsus** and **Jakob Böhme**, or an infinite number from supreme poets like **Dante** and **Shakespeare**. For an analytical commentator to boast that he surpasses his visionary masters is as absurd as **holding a flickering candle in the blazing midday sunshine**.

### 2. Detailed Summary for an 8-Year-Old Learner

In **Section 11**, William Blake explains why copying and organizing rules is never as great as using your own real imagination:

1. **The Table of Contents vs. A Real Story:** The writer **Swedenborg** bragged that his books were full of brand-new ideas, because he loved making neat checklists ("systematic reasoning"). However, Blake says Swedenborg just wrote an **Index or Table of Contents** of ideas that great poets had already written long ago!

2. **The Man with the Pet Monkey:** Blake tells a funny story about a man who carried a monkey around for a show, and because he was a little bit smarter than the monkey, he bragged that he was smarter than seven wise grown-ups! Swedenborg did the same thing: just because he noticed that grumpy church hypocrites were silly, he thought he was the smartest person in the world—and because he was too proud to talk to the fiery, creative **Devils** and only talked to the rule-following **Angels**, he repeated all the old mistakes.

3. **Holding a Candle in the Sunshine:** Great imaginative creators like **Paracelsus, Jakob Böhme, Dante, and Shakespeare** are like the giant, blazing **Sun** in the sky. People who just summarize, copy, and make rulebooks out of their ideas are only **holding a tiny candle in the bright sunshine**—so they should never brag that they know better than the true artists who gave them their light!
